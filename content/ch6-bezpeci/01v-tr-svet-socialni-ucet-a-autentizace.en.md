Someone took over your friend’s account during the night. They changed the password, the email and the name, and now from her profile they are messaging everyone to send them the code they are about to receive.

How does it happen? An **account** is your **identity** in a service, and the network recognises that it is you by your password. This check is called **authentication**. Whoever knows the password is you as far as the network is concerned, even if it is a stranger on the other side of the world.

The most common ways in: the password was the same as on another website that someone hacked (**a leak from one service** puts the others at risk too). It was short and could be guessed, like *name2012*. Or your friend typed it in herself on a fake login page. And then there is what spreads by itself: a message comes from the stolen account saying *send me the code you just got, I put in your number by mistake*. That code is really the check for taking over the account of whoever received it.

Defence:

1. **A long password**, for example a sentence of several words, and **a different one for every service**. A **password manager** will store them.
2. **Two-factor authentication (2FA)**: to the password you **know**, you add a code from the phone you **have**. A stolen password is then not enough on its own. An authenticator app is best; a code by text message is better than nothing.
3. **Never give the code to anyone.** A verification code is the key to your account. No friend and no “support team” needs it.
4. **A backup email, phone number and backup codes.** If your account is stolen, they are how you get it back.

If it happens: change your password at once, report the account takeover through the network’s page, and message your friends another way so they don’t send anything.

**Try it!** Switch on 2FA for the account you use most. What method does the network offer you: text message, an authenticator app or a security key? And where are your backup codes stored?
