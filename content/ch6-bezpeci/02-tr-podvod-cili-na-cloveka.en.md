A text arrives: “Your parcel is waiting for a payment of €1.29.” You happen to be expecting something. Do you click?

The most common attack doesn't break the computer. It breaks **you**. The message plays on **hurry**, **fear**, **reward** or **trust**, so that you act before you think. This is called **social engineering**. A message that tries to lure out passwords and details is **phishing**.

Decide on the messages in the mini-game below the text. For each one ask: **is it pushing me** (hurry, fear, reward, trust)? And **does it want a password, a code, money or logging in through a link**?

The advice “you can spot a scam by its bad grammar” no longer works. AI writes a message without mistakes and imitates a voice too. More reliable signs are **pressure** and a **request** for a password, a code, money or logging in through a link.

The defence has three steps. **Stop.** **Check it another way**: call a number you know, open the app yourself. And **never give your passwords or codes to anyone**, not to support, not to a friend.

**Try it!** Make a “scam bingo” card with your classmates: hurry, a prize, a request for a code, a strange address, an impersonal greeting… Who collects the most squares from real spam in the coming weeks? (Collect, don't click!)

> **Watch out, a common mistake:** “It wouldn't work on me.” A scam doesn't target your reason, it targets your emotions: hurry, fear, joy. And today's scam messages are written by AI without mistakes, so you can't spot them by their mistakes.
