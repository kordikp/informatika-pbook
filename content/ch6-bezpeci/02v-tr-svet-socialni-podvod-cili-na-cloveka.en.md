“Congratulations, you’ve won an iPhone! Just pay €2 postage within 24 hours.” The message comes from an account that looks like a shop’s official profile: logo, photos, thousands of followers.

A fake competition is a classic case of **social engineering**, an attack that doesn’t break into a computer, but into **you**. It plays on three emotions: **reward** (an iPhone), **hurry** (within 24 hours) and sometimes **fear** (or you lose the prize). The postage is really a card payment on a fraudulent page, which is called **phishing**: it looks like a shop, but your card details go to the scammer. Often with a subscription you don’t know about, too.

![A fake competition and its warning signs](images/svet-socialni-soutez.en.svg)

You used to be able to spot a scam by its spelling mistakes. Today **AI** writes it without a single mistake, makes a profile with photos and replies in the comments like a person. The voice in a video can be **cloned**, and an ad in which a celebrity recommends the competition can be a **deepfake**. You can tell nothing from how it looks. You can tell by **what it wants from you**: to pay, to enter your card, a password or a code, and right now.

Defence has three steps:

1. **Stop.** Hurry is a sign of a scam, not a reason to hurry.
2. **Check it another way.** Find the shop’s official website yourself, not through the link, and see whether it is really running the competition. A profile set up last week with bought followers proves nothing.
3. **Never give out** passwords, codes from text messages or card details. You never pay for a real prize.

**Try it!** Find a competition on social media and check whether it is real. Who is running it, how long has the profile existed, and does the official website mention it? What does the competition want from you?
