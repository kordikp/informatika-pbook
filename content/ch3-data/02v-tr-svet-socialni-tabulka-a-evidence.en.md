In the EU, every big network has to give you a copy of your data if you ask. You get a file with tables. And in them is your life, row by row.

In your account settings you will find *Download your data*. A few hours or days later an archive arrives. Inside is your watch history, likes, searches and logins. For example:

| date and time | video | creator | watched (s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 Nov 2026 21:14 | skateboard trick | @skatejack | 47 |
| 3 Nov 2026 21:15 | pancake recipe | @bakingwithgran | 3 |
| 3 Nov 2026 21:15 | falling off a bike | @failclips | 61 |

That is a **record-keeping system**. **Each row is one record**, one view, and **each column one property**: when, what, from whom and for how long. Data like this is **structured**: every item has its place. Your comments and messages, on the other hand, are **unstructured** data, free text that is harder to search.

The network keeps to **rules for entering data**: the time always in the same **format**, each video only once and under its own number (**no duplicates**), and in every row the **required items**, when and what. Only because of that can it calculate across billions of rows.

And you can calculate too. Open the table in a spreadsheet and **sort** it by the *watched* column: at the top you will see what you got stuck on the longest. A **filter** on one creator shows how many of their videos you have seen. The **sum** function adds up the seconds for a week, and **count** counts the views. Exactly from calculations like these the network works out what you are interested in. Pancakes after three seconds? It won’t offer you many more of those.

**Try it!** Work it out from the table above: how many seconds did these three views last altogether? Going by the numbers, which video would the network offer to more viewers, and why?
