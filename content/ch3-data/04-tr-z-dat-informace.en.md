Your phone says: “40% more screen time this week!” Is that a lot or a little? And what is it calculating it from?

Data says nothing by itself. You get **information** from it when you ask a **question**, choose a suitable **chart** and interpret the result carefully. You can cheat at every one of those steps.

Three charts “from the papers” and three tricks. **The axis doesn't start at zero**: school A scores 82% in a test, school B 78%, and with bars starting at 76% it looks three times as much. **Picked days**: the chart shows only the first three days when a video was growing and hides that interest then fell. **A link instead of a cause**: bigger fires have more firefighters and more damage. But the firefighters don't cause the damage. Both are caused by the size of the fire.

Working with data has a cycle: **question → collection → cleaning → analysis → chart → interpretation**. It starts with a question, not with a chart.

Choose the chart by the question. **Change over time** is shown by a line, **comparison** by bars. With bars, check whether the axis starts at zero. Ask **where the data comes from and what is missing from it**.

And above all: when two things grow together, it is a **link (correlation)**. It doesn't yet mean that one **causes** the other **(causation)**. Often something else is behind both.

**Try it!** Take your marks in one subject and draw them twice: once honestly (the whole scale), once “for your parents” (a cut-off axis, a picked bit of the term). Which trick works best? Now you'll never miss it again.

> **Watch out, a common mistake:** “Numbers don't lie.” Numbers maybe not, but charts do. A cut-off axis, left-out data or a link passed off as a cause turn the same numbers into a different story.
