A step-counting app shows you a graph: on Thursday it looks as if you hardly moved. It looks like a slump. But is that really so?

Apps on your phone collect lots of **data**: steps, sleep, screen time. Data on its own says nothing. **Information** is only the answer to a **question**, and the question comes first: *do I walk less during the week than at the weekend? do I sleep worse when I look at my phone in the evening?* Only then does it make sense to choose a graph.

**The graph is chosen according to the question.** **Change** over time is shown by a line graph, for example steps day by day. **Comparison** by a bar chart: the weekend against the week. **Share** by a pie chart: how much of your screen time went on games.

![The same data, two axes: a slump or almost nothing?](images/svet-aplikace-graf.en.svg)

Watch out for **misleading axes**. When the axis doesn’t start at zero but at 7,000 steps, the difference between 9,000 and 8,000 looks like a fall into an abyss. In reality it is one ninth. Apps like to cut axes short so that the graph looks interesting.

And watch out for conclusions. The app notices that on days when you sleep less, you also have more screen time. **Correlation**, two things going together, is not yet **causation**, when one causes the other. Maybe your phone keeps you awake. Or these are the days before a test: you are nervous, you don’t sleep and you scroll. A third thing can cause both.

Finally, ask **where the data comes from and what is missing from it**. A phone left in your bag doesn’t count steps. Football in the gym isn’t in the graph at all, and Thursday’s “slump” may simply be the day you left your phone in the changing room.

**Try it!** Find the statistics for steps or screen time on your phone. Ask yourself one question and see whether the graph really answers it. Where does its axis start?
