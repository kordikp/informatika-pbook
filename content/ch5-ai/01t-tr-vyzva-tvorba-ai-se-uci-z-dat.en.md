The generator draws a scientist as an older man with glasses in a white coat. Makes sense, you think, there are most of those in the data. But a calculation shows that this “typical scientist” is a minority in the data.

<!-- task -->

A made-up training set has 10,000 pictures labelled “scientist”. Of them, 70 % are men, 80 % wear a white coat, 60 % wear glasses and 65 % are over 50. Assume the features are not related to each other (they are **independent**).

1. For each feature, the generator picks the most common option. What percentage of scientists in the data have **all four** features at once?
2. A fair generator would draw a man in 70 % of cases. What is the probability that 4 pictures are all men just by chance?
3. You generate 20 pictures and all 20 show a man. How likely would that be with a fair generator? Is it evidence that the model **amplifies** a bias?

**Success criterion:** three numbers rounded to whole percentages (for the third, the order of magnitude is enough) and one sentence of conclusion.

<!-- hint -->

For independent features, you find the probability that all of them hold at once by multiplying the individual probabilities.

<!-- hint -->

Four men in a row: 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7.

<!-- hint -->

You can work out 0.7 to the power of twenty step by step: 0.7² = 0.49, 0.7⁴ ≈ 0.24, 0.7⁸ ≈ 0.058, 0.7¹⁶ ≈ 0.0033 and finally × 0.7⁴.

<!-- solution -->

**1.** 0.7 × 0.8 × 0.6 × 0.65 ≈ 0.22, so about **22 %**. “An older man with glasses in a coat” matches only about a fifth of the data. The generator does not show the typical scientist from the data, but **the most common value of each feature separately**. When you put them together, you get a combination that is rare in the data.

**2.** 0.7⁴ ≈ 0.24, so **24 %**. Four men out of four proves nothing yet; it happens roughly every fourth try.

**3.** 0.7²⁰ ≈ 0.0008, so about **0.08 %**, less than once in a thousand tries. That is strong evidence that the model does not draw according to the data, but sharpens the stereotype even more.

**Conclusion:** amplification is measured by counting over a large sample, not from one picture. You know the same principle from the trip “Inside a chatbot”: when a model always takes the most likely choice, variety gets lost.

<!-- more -->

Design your own audit. Choose a job and write down in advance what you will count (age, gender, clothes, setting) and what result would match reality. Image generators are usually for ages 13 and up, so younger kids do the audit with a parent or on pictures generated by an older brother or sister or a teacher. Put the results in a table and compare them with real statistics.
