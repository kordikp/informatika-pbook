Five websites say the same thing. That’s five confirmations, right? Not necessarily. If they all copy from one, you have one source in five coats. For a school report about the river Mlýnka, you now untangle who got it from whom.

<!-- task -->

The claim: **“Beavers are living in the river Mlýnka again.”** Seven sources turned up:

1. **Chatbot**: an answer with a link to an article in the Village Newsletter.
2. **Village Newsletter**: “As the village website says…”
3. **Village website**: links to a nature conservation report.
4. **Nature conservation report**: its own count, camera traps, spring 2025.
5. **An angler’s blog**: his own photos of gnawed trees by the river.
6. **A social media video**: “I read it on some angler’s blog.”
7. **School noticeboard**: text copied from the chatbot’s answer.

1. Draw arrows for “who got it from whom”. How many **independent** sources are there, and which are they?
2. Which source will you check first, and why?
3. Twist: the nature conservation report says the beavers live in the **neighbouring stream**, not in the Mlýnka. The village website got it wrong. How many independent confirmations are left now? Write one honest sentence for the report.

<!-- hint -->

From each source, follow the arrows until you reach a source that quotes nobody and has its own observations.

<!-- hint -->

Sources that lead to the same end count as one. The number of repetitions doesn’t matter.

<!-- hint -->

After the twist, follow which chains led to the nature conservation report. What happens to everything that copied from it?

<!-- solution -->

**1.** Arrows: noticeboard → chatbot → Newsletter → village website → nature conservation report. Video → angler’s blog. Only **two** sources have their own observations: the **nature conservation report** and the **angler’s blog**. Seven sources, two confirmations.

**2.** The nature conservation report. It is at the start of the longest chain, and it describes the method (count, camera traps) and the date. Who says it, how they know and why: it answers everything.

**3.** The whole chain of noticeboard, chatbot, Newsletter and village website falls, because it comes from a wrong reading. There is **one** confirmation left, the angler’s blog, and that is only indirect evidence (gnaw marks). An honest sentence might be: “An angler shows gnawed trees on his blog, which suggest beavers. There is no official confirmation for the Mlýnka yet; nature conservation reports beavers in the neighbouring stream.”

<!-- more -->

You can draw the same graph for any message that reaches you. Take one chain message from your class group chat (without classmates’ names) and try to get to its root. You’ll often find that there is no root. Then make a rule for yourself: how many independent confirmations do you want for a message you forward, and how many for something in a school report? And why is it different for each?
