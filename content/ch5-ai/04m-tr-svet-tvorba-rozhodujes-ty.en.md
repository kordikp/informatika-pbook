You generated a picture for a school poster, and it is really good. Before you send it out into the world, ask yourself three questions. Artists, courts and lawmakers are asking themselves the same ones today.

**1. Whose works did the model learn from?** Generators were trained on billions of pictures from the internet, largely on the works of real artists who never agreed to it. That is why they can draw “in the style of” a particular illustrator: they have seen that illustrator’s work. Generating in the style of a living artist and passing it off as your own work is a foul. Their style is their livelihood.

**2. Is it true?** A picture that looks like a photo is a claim to the viewer: *this happened*. Before you use it, **check** what it shows. For a picture in a school report on “what a castle looked like in 1400”, check it in a **second, independent source**, and ask who says so, how they know and why. AI will happily draw you a castle that never stood.

**3. Will you say that AI made it?** Yes. One sentence is enough: *AI created the picture from my description; I chose it and edited it*. The audience has the right to know whether they are looking at a photo of a real event or at something a machine made. The big platforms already require AI content to be labelled, and in the EU it is compulsory for deepfakes that look realistic.

**When not to use AI at all:** when you are supposed to learn something, for example in art class, where you are practising drawing. When you would be uploading **sensitive data**, photos of classmates who didn’t agree. And when the picture would **make decisions about people** or make fun of them. A deepfake of a classmate isn’t a joke; it is bullying.

AI is a tool and it gives suggestions. **You decide and you are responsible**: which of the results you use, what you edit and what you throw away.

**Try it!** Go through your feed and find three pictures where you aren’t sure whether a person or AI made them. Are they labelled? Write an honest label for one of them.
