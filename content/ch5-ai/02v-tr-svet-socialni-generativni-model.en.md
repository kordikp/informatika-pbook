A video appears in your feed: a famous YouTuber promises to double everyone’s money. It speaks in his voice, moves his mouth, blinks. But he never said any of it.

It is a **deepfake**, a video made by a **generative model**. It works much like a chatbot. A chatbot predicts the next word (**token**) by what is **probable**. A video model predicts the next frame and the movement of the mouth; a voice model predicts the next sound. A few minutes of recordings from YouTube are enough for it to learn how the YouTuber sounds and what faces he makes.

It looks convincing because it is probable. Not because it is true. **Fluency is not truth.** A text model will just as confidently make up a quote, and that is called a **hallucination**. A video model makes up a whole event.

Filters on social networks work on the same principle: they smooth your skin, make your eyes bigger, change your voice. It is fun as long as you know it is a filter. The problem is that in a finished video you may not be able to tell.

How do you spot a deepfake? By its mistakes, less and less: the models get better every month. It is more reliable to ask:

- **What does it want from me?** Money, a click, a share “before they delete it”? That is a warning, however lifelike the video is.
- **Where is it from?** Is it on that person’s official account? Is it in the news?
- **Does it make sense?** Why would a famous YouTuber give money away to strangers?

And one more thing: your voice and your face in videos are material too. The more of them you have in public, the easier it is for a model to learn from them.

**Try it!** Find an example of a well-known deepfake that was in the news. How did people give it away? And could you have spotted it even without that mistake?
