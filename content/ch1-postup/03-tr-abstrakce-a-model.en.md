The map on your phone only shows you streets and an arrow on the way. You don't see trees or the colour of the houses. Why doesn't that bother you?

A **model** is a simplified version of reality for a particular purpose. It leaves out everything you don't need for that purpose. What matters **depends on the purpose**: a tourist needs a different map from a driver.

Take a city with an underground. A bird's-eye view shows houses, trees, the river and the streets: far too much for changing lines. A street plan leaves out the houses and trees, which suits someone on foot. The underground map leaves out the streets and real distances too. What's left are the stations and the connections, and you can see straight away where to change. Finally a **graph**: just the stations and the lines between them.

The underground map **is not accurate**, and that is exactly why it is useful. It has no streets and no real distances, so it is easy to change lines with it.

Lots of models are **graphs**: the things are **nodes** and the connections between them are **edges**. Stations and connections. Classmates and friendships. Chapters and how they follow on. Even the map of this book is a graph.

No model is complete. It is always a **choice** of what to leave out.

**Try it!** Describe the way from school to your home to a friend. Notice everything you leave out without thinking (the colour of the houses, the number of lamp posts, the cars by the kerb) and what you keep (the turns, the landmarks). You have just made an abstraction. Now try this: what would you keep if you were describing the same route to a driver? A different purpose, different details that matter.

> **Watch out, a common mistake:** “The more detail a model has, the better it is.” A good model is actually as simple as possible for its purpose. The underground map doesn't show streets, and that is exactly why it is so easy to travel with.
