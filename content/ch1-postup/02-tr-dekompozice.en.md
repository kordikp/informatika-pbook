In a week you have a test on three chapters. Learning everything at once on Sunday night doesn't work. How do you split it up?

Hardly anyone solves a big problem in one go. **You break it into smaller parts** that you can solve one by one, and then put them together. This is called **decomposition**.

First get the **task** clear: what you have, what you want and what the limits are. Then break the task down. Some parts **depend** on each other: other parts have to be done first.

Your class is making a video about the school. You have 3 weeks, 4 people and a phone on a tripod, and you may only film during breaks. The breakdown: **Script** (choose a topic → write the script), **Filming** (borrow the phone and tripod → shoot the footage) and **Editing** (edit the footage → add subtitles and music → show the video to the class). An arrow means “only after that”.

The breakdown shows two things. The parts **are not the same size** and they **follow on from each other**: you can't shoot the footage without a script. Tasks that don't depend on each other can be done by several people at once.

In programming, a named part of a solution is called a **subprogram** (or function). The game Snake is the snake's movement, the food, the crashes and the score. You write and test each part separately.

**Try it!** Take a task you keep putting off (a presentation, training, tidying your room) and write it out as the smallest possible steps, so small that none takes more than 15 minutes. Then do just the first one. It works suspiciously well.

> **Watch out, a common mistake:** “Breaking a task down means cutting it into equal pieces.” The parts are usually different sizes and follow on from each other: until you have a script, you have nothing to film.
